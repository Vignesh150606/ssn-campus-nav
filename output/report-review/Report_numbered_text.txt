L01  ABSTRACT
L02  Many road collisions originate from safety-critical information that
L03  exists in the traffic environment but does not reach the driver in
L04  time: a stationary truck beyond a curve, an oncoming vehicle
L05  occluded by the vehicle being overtaken, or a cascade of successive
L06  braking events. This work investigates whether vehicle-to-vehicle
L07  (V2V) communication, combined with a lightweight neural network
L08  executed on the vehicle’s embedded hardware, can provide timely
L09  and actionable warnings in four such scenarios.
L10  The scenarios were modelled in SUMO using the Veins and
L11  OMNeT++ co-simulation framework. With cooperative warning
L12  enabled, three scenarios that resulted in collisions in the baseline
L13  configuration were completed without contact, while the fourth
L14  maintained larger inter-vehicle gaps. A synthetic dataset of 10,000
L15  samples was generated and used to train an 11–16–16–2 fully
L16  connected network (498 parameters) that jointly predicts collision
L17  risk and recommended speed, achieving test errors of 0.039 (risk)
L18  and 0.134 m/s (speed). The trained model was exported as C code
L19  and deployed on an ESP32, with a mean inference time of 57 µs

=== Printed page iv | PDF page 4 ===
L01  and a maximum deviation of 3.3 × 10⁻⁶ from the reference
L02  implementation across 1,215 inference requests. Two ESP32-S3
L03  nodes now exchange vehicle state over ESP-NOW at 10 Hz.
L04  In closed-loop braking tests, the proposed system reduced closing
L05  speed at impact by roughly 93%, from 2.7 to 0.2 m/s, although a
L06  collision was not entirely eliminated. This is attributed to the
L07  independent estimation of risk and speed and to oscillation at close
L08  range, both of which motivate a supervisory controller. Phase II will
L09  add a supervisory controller, a custom PCB, a broadcast-beacon
L10  link, and RC-vehicle validation.

L01  CHAPTER 1
L02  INTRODUCTION
L03  1.1 Background and motivation
L04  Many serious road collisions come down to a driver learning
L05  about a hazard too late. The hazard is usually easy to understand once it
L06  has been seen: a truck stopped just beyond a bend, an oncoming car
L07  hidden behind the lorry that a driver is trying to overtake, a line of cars
L08  braking one after another on an open road. In every one of these cases
L09  the information already exists somewhere on the road. It just is not in the
L10  line of sight of the vehicle that needs it.
L11  Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication is a way of passing
L12  that information along. Each vehicle broadcasts its position, speed and
L13  braking state, and nearby vehicles pick the message up directly, with no
L14  cellular network in between. SAE J2735 defines the Basic Safety
L15  Message used for this, while IEEE 802.11p and the IEEE 1609 family
L16  cover the radio and networking layers (Kenney 2011). If a message
L17  arrives while the hazard is still hidden, a following driver can gain
L18  several seconds that a camera or radar could never give.
L19  Receiving a message, though, does not tell the vehicle what to
L20  do about it. It still has to turn the sender's state into a judgement of how
L21  dangerous the situation is and how fast it should be going. That
L22  judgement must be made on board, within a fraction of a control cycle,
L23  and it must stay sensible when messages arrive late or not at all. Machine
L24  learning suits the task, but most of the V2X learning work reviewed for

=== Printed page 2 | PDF page 17 ===
L01  this project runs on servers or workstations. The question here was how
L02  small the estimator could be made, and whether it could still be trusted
L03  once it was moved onto a microcontroller.
L04  The project therefore brings together three pieces: highway
L05  scenarios simulated with SUMO and Veins, a compact neural network
L06  that returns a collision risk and a recommended speed, and an ESP32
L07  prototype in which two boards exchange vehicle state over ESP-NOW
L08  and run the network locally. By the end of Phase I the network, with 498
L09  parameters and about 2 KB of weights, ran on the ESP32 in roughly 57
L10  µs per inference, and two ESP32 boards could exchange state and close a
L11  simple speed-control loop.
L12  1.2 Problem statement
L13  A vehicle receives a state message from another vehicle about
L14  ten times a second. It combines that message with its own speed, the gap
L15  to the other vehicle and some measure of how reliable the link is. From
L16  these eleven quantities it must estimate the probability that the situation
L17  ends in contact, and advise a speed that avoids it. The estimate has to be
L18  produced on the vehicle itself, so that no decision depends on a remote
L19  server.
L20  The information is incomplete for two reasons. One is
L21  geometry: a bend, a hillside or another vehicle can hide the hazard from
L22  the on-board sensors. The other is the radio link, where messages can be
L23  delayed, dropped or relayed through several hops, so the sender's state is
L24  already out of date when it is used. For this reason the model receives the
L25  packet delivery ratio, the age of the last message and a hop count

=== Printed page 3 | PDF page 18 ===
L01  alongside the kinematic quantities, and it is expected to grow more
L02  cautious as the link gets worse.
L03  Producing a number is only part of the job. The value must
L04  mean the same thing in firmware as it did in training, it must arrive early
L05  enough to be useful, and any speed command built from it must be one
L06  that a real vehicle can follow. The oscillation at close range seen in the
L07  closed-loop tests (Chapter 7) shows that a fast, numerically exact
L08  inference is not enough by itself.

L01  CHAPTER 2
L02  REVIEW OF LITERATURE
L03  2.1 Overview
L04  The literature relevant to this project falls into four groups:
L05  learning-based collision prediction from V2X data, message
L06  dissemination and traffic-level estimation over V2V links, the simulation
L07  and communication tools used to study them, and machine learning on
L08  small embedded devices. Four papers were read in full, and the rest are
L09  placed using their abstracts and bibliographic records.
L10  2.2 Collision prediction from V2X data
L11  Ribeiro et al. (2023) predict collisions involving a
L12  motorcyclist, whom they treat as a vulnerable road user. Communication
L13  data are generated with the Veins framework, which couples SUMO with
L14  the ns-3 network simulator, and stacked unidirectional LSTM layers are
L15  trained on the resulting sequences. In the first scenario the model
L16  predicted 96% of the collisions with an average prediction time of 4.53 s;
L17  in the second it predicted 95% with 4.44 s. The cost was a high number
L18  of false positives (78 and 68 events), and the correct-decision percentage
L19  stayed at 41% and 43%. The authors conclude that the output is good
L20  enough to warn a rider but not to trigger automatic emergency braking.
L21  Two points from that study influenced this project. The false
L22  positives are a reminder that a predictor with high recall needs a plan for
L23  the times it is wrong, which is why minimum gaps and contact outcomes

=== Printed page 9 | PDF page 24 ===
L01  are reported in simulation here, not just regression error. And an LSTM
L02  that reads a time series is a heavier object than a feed-forward network
L03  that reads a single state vector. The 4.5 s figure is also worth reading
L04  carefully: it measures how far ahead of a collision the prediction was
L05  made, not how long the computation took, so it cannot be set against the
L06  microsecond inference times reported later.
L07  Zou et al. (2023) consider an automated vehicle approaching a
L08  pedestrian who is completely hidden by vehicles in the adjacent lane. A
L09  baseline strategy is compared with one that uses V2V messages from the
L10  neighbouring vehicles. Without V2V, the time to collision is close to
L11  zero when the pedestrian is detected, and the vehicle brakes hard but still
L12  hits the pedestrian. With V2V it can brake in proportion to the hazard,
L13  the time to collision stays larger and the pedestrian crosses safely. The
L14  experimental design, a baseline run and a V2V run from identical starting
L15  conditions, is the one followed in Chapter 7.
L16  Other recent work approaches the same family of problems
L17  from different directions. Ben-Mosbah et al. (2024) study the electronic
L18  emergency brake light application over 5G NR V2V, the same situation
L19  as the braking-chain scenario here but with a cellular sidelink radio.
L20  Farhat et al. (2024) propose a cooperative collision-avoidance system for
L21  vehicular communication based on deep learning. Park et al. (2025)
L22  combine C-V2X with camera-based object detection in a client–server
L23  arrangement and report a mean broadcast latency of 9.24 ms for obstacle
L24  information on a test track. None of these runs its model on a
L25  microcontroller, which is the gap this project tries to fill.

=== Printed page 10 | PDF page 25 ===
L01  2.3 Message dissemination and traffic estimation
L02  Li et al. (2022) look at the load that safety messages place on
L03  the network. Their end–edge–cloud architecture lets edge nodes predict
L04  future vehicle trajectories and the density of vehicles along them. A
L05  warning is then sent only to vehicles expected to pass through the
L06  affected area, and the system switches between V2V and V2I delivery
L07  depending on connectivity. On a real-world VANET mobility dataset it
L08  outperformed schemes that ignore the predicted trajectory density. That
L09  work is about who should receive a message, whereas this project is
L10  about what the receiver does with it. The two fit together, since an
L11  estimator can only act on state that is still fresh.
L12  Wang et al. (2019) detect road congestion with V2V
L13  messages. A fuzzy controller built on vehicle speed and traffic density
L14  grades the local congestion level, neighbouring vehicles are asked for
L15  theirs, and a hypothesis test over a large sub-sample gives a regional
L16  estimate. In simulation the detection accuracy was 5.5% higher than a
L17  geomagnetic-coil method and 7.5% higher than the CoTEC method,
L18  while the V2V network overhead was 90.8% lower than CoTEC.
L19  Congestion and imminent collision are different targets, since a slow,
L20  dense queue need not contain any conflict and a collision can arise on an
L21  empty road. What carries over from this paper is the idea of computing a
L22  compact local estimate on each vehicle and exchanging only what
L23  neighbours need.
L24  Zardosht et al. (2017) use V2V messages for another purpose,
L25  deciding when a driver should be rerouted after an accident. Their
L26  module estimates the accident duration, takes traffic congestion and the
L27  type of area into account, and was evaluated in city and highway

=== Printed page 11 | PDF page 26 ===
L01  simulations with different proportions of V2V-equipped vehicles. It
L02  reduced waiting and travel times. The time scale is minutes rather than
L03  seconds, so it does not compete with this work, but it shows the same
L04  pattern: a small decision module on each vehicle, fed by neighbour data.
L05  2.4 Simulation, communication and embedded tools
L06  The simulation side of the project rests on a well-established
L07  tool chain. SUMO is an open-source microscopic traffic simulator in
L08  which each vehicle follows a car-following model on a defined road
L09  network (Lopez et al. 2018). Its TraCI interface lets an external program
L10  read vehicle state and issue commands at every simulation step (Wegener
L11  et al. 2008), which is how the four scenario controllers are written.
L12  OMNeT++ is a discrete-event network simulator (Varga and Hornig
L13  2008), and Veins couples it to SUMO so that vehicle motion and
L14  wireless events influence each other (Sommer et al. 2011).
L15  For the communication layer the project borrows ideas from
L16  the U.S. DSRC family. IEEE 802.11p provides the radio, IEEE 1609 the
L17  networking services, and SAE J2735 and J2945/1 define the message set
L18  and its performance requirements (Kenney 2011). The prototype does not
L19  implement these stacks. It uses ESP-NOW, a connectionless protocol
L20  from Espressif that sends short frames directly between ESP32 boards
L21  without an access point (Espressif Systems n.d.). The choice was a
L22  practical one, not a standards-based one, and Chapter 3 explains how
L23  closely the prototype follows the standards.
L24  On the embedded side, TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers
L25  shows that neural-network inference can fit in tens of kilobytes (David et
L26  al. 2021), and Pohuliai et al. (2026) compare several TinyML models for

=== Printed page 12 | PDF page 27 ===
L01  sensor classification on the ESP32. No runtime library was used here. A
L02  network with two hidden layers of this size is simple enough to export as
L03  plain C arrays and a few loops, which keeps the firmware transparent and
L04  makes it easy to check against the training framework.

L09  the fast vehicle, starting at 4.2 m/s with a gap of 1.8 m to the other bot

L16  6.3 Link-quality inputs
L17  Three of the model inputs describe the link, and the firmware
L18  derives them from the packets themselves. The packet delivery ratio is
L19  the number of packets received divided by the number expected, where
L20  the expected count grows by the jump in sequence number each time a
L21  packet arrives. Message age is the time since the last packet was
L22  received. Hop count is read from the packet and is always 1 in the two-
L23  board set-up. If nothing has been heard for 2 s, the bot treats the other as
L24  silent and substitutes a zero speed, zero acceleration, an age of 250 ms
L25  and a single hop.
L26  These definitions are adequate for a first demonstration, but
L27  they differ from the training data in three ways that will matter in Phase
L28  II. First, the firmware PDR is cumulative since start-up, whereas the
L29  dataset used a sliding window, so one early loss lowers it for a long time.
L30  Second, message age is measured from reception and not from the
L31  sender's timestamp, so it leaves out the time the packet spent in transit.

=== Printed page 38 | PDF page 53 ===
L01  Third, a bot is considered alive during the first 2 s after boot even though
L02  no packet has been received, so for that interval the model is fed
L03  placeholder values for the other vehicle.
L04  The occlusion flag is a heuristic. It is set when the other bot is
L05  moving faster than 0.3 m/s and the gap is more than 4.5 m. The ToF
L06  sensor assumed in the dataset reaches 4.0 m, so a gap beyond 4.5 m
L07  means that the sensor could not have seen the other vehicle while the
L08  V2V link could. In the present tests the gap is itself simulated, so the flag
L09  shows how the input would be set and does not represent a real sensing
L10  event.
L11  6.4 From point-to-point exchange to a broadcast beacon
L12  Pairing two boards by MAC address was a conscious
L13  simplification. The goal at the end of Phase I was to show that two
L14  ESP32 boards could exchange vehicle state over ESP-NOW and use it in
L15  the model, and that goal was met: both bots ran the loop for the full
L16  duration of the tests and responded to each other's state.
L17  A road is not a pair of vehicles, however, and the planned
L18  arrangement is different. Each vehicle will broadcast a beacon, and any
L19  vehicles within range will receive it and respond. ESP-NOW supports a
L20  broadcast address, so the radio itself needs no change, but the firmware
L21  will have to be reorganised around it. It will need a table of nearby
L22  senders keyed by identity, with PDR and message age kept separately for
L23  each; a rule for choosing which neighbour to treat as the conflict vehicle;
L24  and filtering by signal strength or distance, so that distant vehicles do not
L25  clutter the table. These points are listed as Phase II work in Chapter 8.

L12  A 60 s dynamic-drive run produced 600 requests, with a mean
L13  inference time of 57.2 µs and a maximum of 61 µs. The speed trace in
L14  the closed-loop braking figure shows repeated fluctuations once the
L15  vehicles are close. The controller internals needed to explain this were
L16  not logged. The advised speed depends on a noisy-looking function of
L17  gap and closing speed, and a controller that follows it with a fixed gain
L18  can end up chasing it. This is a hypothesis, not a result.
L19  The dashboard example reports 248 µs for inference and 31.8
L20  ms for the round trip, and the 248 µs is higher than the serial figures. The
L21  measurement boundaries differ, and the dashboard figure comes from a
L22  single request with Wi-Fi traffic active on the board, so the larger
L23  number may include cache and radio effects. This has not been
L24  investigated further, and the two sets of values should not be compared
L25  directly.

=== Printed page 52 | PDF page 67 ===
L01  7.9 Interpretation
L02  Three findings stand out. First, the model is small and fast:
L03  498 parameters, about 2 KB, and roughly 57 µs per inference, which is a
L04  tiny share of a 100 ms cycle. Second, the board reproduces the reference
L05  network to about 3 × 10−6, so the deployed model is the trained model.
L06  Third, in the sweeps the outputs move in the right direction for both gap
L07  and link quality.
L08  Against that, the closed-loop braking test shows that a fast and
L09  accurate estimator is not yet a safe controller. The risk was low while the
L10  ego vehicle accelerated, contact still occurred, and the speed oscillated at
L11  close range. The dashboard example showed the same problem in a static
L12  form, with a risk of 64.7% and an advised speed above the current one.
L13  These are read as consequences of using two independent heads without
L14  a rule that links them, a point taken up in the next chapter.
L15  The evidence has limits. The risk label comes from a
L16  simulation with assumed parameters, the held-out error is measured on
L17  randomly split rows from the same generator, and the highway runs use a
L18  scripted baseline driver. None of it is a measurement on real vehicles.

L01  CHAPTER 8
L02  DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
L03  8.1 Engineering significance and limitations
L04  The project joins a compact model to an embedded
L05  cooperative prototype and checks it at each junction. In simulation,
L06  cooperative warning changed three of the four outcomes from contact to
L07  no contact and widened the margin in the fourth. On the board, the
L08  network runs in under a tenth of a millisecond and matches its training-
L09  framework reference to about 3 × 10−6. The rest of the chain, from
L10  sensing to actuation, has not been profiled.
L11  The sweeps are useful checks of behaviour. A wider gap
L12  lowers the risk and raises the advised speed, and worse link quality
L13  makes the outputs more cautious. They cover only the chosen input
L14  conditions, however, and do not establish behaviour over every
L15  combination of inputs the network may meet.
L16  The control side needs work. The braking comparison still
L17  ends in contact, the speed oscillates at close range, and the dashboard
L18  example shows the two outputs disagreeing. One contributor in the two-
L19  board firmware may be the rate limit: a speed change of at most 0.08 m/s
L20  per 100 ms tick allows a deceleration of only 0.8 m/s², while the dataset
L21  assumes braking of 1.5 to 3.0 m/s². A controller that cannot brake as hard
L22  as the labels assume will under-react to a high risk. This is an
L23  observation from the code and has not yet been tested.

=== Printed page 54 | PDF page 69 ===
L01  The prototype does not establish GPS or IMU accuracy, motor
L02  response, radio performance on moving vehicles, or the transfer of
L03  highway-scale behaviour to RC-scale motion. Safer roads remain the
L04  intended application and not something this study measured.
L05  8.2 Proposed supervisory controller
L06  A supervisory layer between the model and the actuator is the
L07  most direct response to these findings. It would check the learned advice
L08  against simple physical and link-quality rules before the vehicle acts on
L09  it. This layer has not been implemented.
L10  The first rule concerns stale or missing information. The
L11  present firmware proceeds without a first beacon, and for the first 2 s
L12  after start-up it feeds placeholder values to the model. A supervisor
L13  should define a safe state for that situation, such as a speed cap until a
L14  valid packet arrives, and a message age beyond which the other vehicle
L15  is treated as unknown. The age limit should come from vehicle dynamics
L16  and be tested.
L17  The second rule links the two outputs: if the risk exceeds a
L18  threshold, the commanded speed should not exceed the current speed,
L19  whatever the second head says. The third is a limit on acceleration while
L20  a hazard is flagged, and the fourth a rate limit that matches the vehicle's
L21  real braking capability. Smoothing or hysteresis on the advice could be
L22  added to reduce oscillation, though each adds delay and so has to be tried
L23  against minimum gap and stopping time.
L24  To judge any of this, the existing controller stays as the
L25  baseline, and the experiment is repeated from the same initial conditions,
L26  communication trace and model weights. Only then can a change in

=== Printed page 55 | PDF page 70 ===
L01  outcome be attributed to the new policy, and no improvement is claimed
L02  until that has been done.

L08  8.4 Conclusion
L09  This work built a complete, if small, chain for cooperative
L10  collision estimation. Four highway situations were modelled in SUMO
L11  with Veins and OMNeT++, and in three of them the baseline driver
L12  collided while the driver with V2V information did not. A bot-scale
L13  dataset of 10,000 rows was generated, and an 11–16–16–2 network with
L14  498 parameters was trained on it, reaching test errors of 0.039 for risk
L15  and 0.134 m/s for speed.
L16  The network was exported as C code and run on an ESP32 at
L17  about 57 µs per inference, with a worst-case difference of 3.30 × 10−6
L18  from its reference over 1,215 requests and no communication errors.
L19  Two boards exchanged state over ESP-NOW at 10 Hz and adjusted their
L20  speeds, and a laptop dashboard exercised the model over Wi-Fi.
L21  The braking tests set the limit on what can be concluded.
L22  Closing speed at contact dropped from 2.7 to 0.2 m/s, but contact still
L23  occurred, and earlier than in the open-loop run. Collision avoidance has
L24  therefore not been shown, and the independent outputs and the close-
L25  range oscillation are the first control issues for Phase II. The next steps
L26  are the broadcast beacon, a supervisory controller, and measurements on

=== Printed page 57 | PDF page 72 ===
L01  an RC platform whose speed, gap and radio performance are recorded
L02  independently.
