02: ABSTRACT
03: Many road collisions originate from safety-critical information that
04: exists in the traffic environment but does not reach the driver in
05: time: a stationary truck beyond a curve, an oncoming vehicle
06: occluded by the vehicle being overtaken, or a cascade of successive
07: braking events. This work investigates whether vehicle-to-vehicle
08: (V2V) communication, combined with a lightweight neural network
09: executed on the vehicle’s embedded hardware, can provide timely
10: and actionable warnings in four such scenarios.
11: The scenarios were modelled in SUMO using the Veins and
12: OMNeT++ co-simulation framework. With cooperative warning
13: enabled, three scenarios that resulted in collisions in the baseline
14: configuration were completed without contact, while the fourth
15: maintained larger inter-vehicle gaps. A synthetic dataset of 10,000
16: samples was generated and used to train an 11–16–16–2 fully
17: connected network (498 parameters) that jointly predicts collision
18: risk and recommended speed, achieving test errors of 0.039 (risk)
19: and 0.134 m/s (speed). The trained model was exported as C code
20: and deployed on an ESP32, with a mean inference time of 57 µs

03: INTRODUCTION
04: 1.1 Background and motivation
05: Many serious road collisions come down to a driver learning
06: about a hazard too late. The hazard is usually easy to understand once it
07: has been seen: a truck stopped just beyond a bend, an oncoming car
08: hidden behind the lorry that a driver is trying to overtake, a line of cars
09: braking one after another on an open road. In every one of these cases
10: the information already exists somewhere on the road. It just is not in the
11: line of sight of the vehicle that needs it.
12: Vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication is a way of passing
13: that information along. Each vehicle broadcasts its position, speed and
14: braking state, and nearby vehicles pick the message up directly, with no
15: cellular network in between. SAE J2735 defines the Basic Safety
16: Message used for this, while IEEE 802.11p and the IEEE 1609 family
17: cover the radio and networking layers (Kenney 2011). If a message
18: arrives while the hazard is still hidden, a following driver can gain
19: several seconds that a camera or radar could never give.
20: Receiving a message, though, does not tell the vehicle what to
21: do about it. It still has to turn the sender's state into a judgement of how
22: dangerous the situation is and how fast it should be going. That
23: judgement must be made on board, within a fraction of a control cycle,
24: and it must stay sensible when messages arrive late or not at all. Machine
25: learning suits the task, but most of the V2X learning work reviewed for

02: this project runs on servers or workstations. The question here was how
03: small the estimator could be made, and whether it could still be trusted
04: once it was moved onto a microcontroller.
05: The project therefore brings together three pieces: highway
06: scenarios simulated with SUMO and Veins, a compact neural network
07: that returns a collision risk and a recommended speed, and an ESP32
08: prototype in which two boards exchange vehicle state over ESP-NOW
09: and run the network locally. By the end of Phase I the network, with 498
10: parameters and about 2 KB of weights, ran on the ESP32 in roughly 57
11: µs per inference, and two ESP32 boards could exchange state and close a
12: simple speed-control loop.
13: 1.2 Problem statement
14: A vehicle receives a state message from another vehicle about
15: ten times a second. It combines that message with its own speed, the gap
16: to the other vehicle and some measure of how reliable the link is. From
17: these eleven quantities it must estimate the probability that the situation
18: ends in contact, and advise a speed that avoids it. The estimate has to be
19: produced on the vehicle itself, so that no decision depends on a remote
20: server.
21: The information is incomplete for two reasons. One is
22: geometry: a bend, a hillside or another vehicle can hide the hazard from
23: the on-board sensors. The other is the radio link, where messages can be
24: delayed, dropped or relayed through several hops, so the sender's state is
25: already out of date when it is used. For this reason the model receives the
26: packet delivery ratio, the age of the last message and a hop count

03: REVIEW OF LITERATURE
04: 2.1 Overview
05: The literature relevant to this project falls into four groups:
06: learning-based collision prediction from V2X data, message
07: dissemination and traffic-level estimation over V2V links, the simulation
08: and communication tools used to study them, and machine learning on
09: small embedded devices. Four papers were read in full, and the rest are
10: placed using their abstracts and bibliographic records.
11: 2.2 Collision prediction from V2X data
12: Ribeiro et al. (2023) predict collisions involving a
13: motorcyclist, whom they treat as a vulnerable road user. Communication
14: data are generated with the Veins framework, which couples SUMO with
15: the ns-3 network simulator, and stacked unidirectional LSTM layers are
16: trained on the resulting sequences. In the first scenario the model
17: predicted 96% of the collisions with an average prediction time of 4.53 s;
18: in the second it predicted 95% with 4.44 s. The cost was a high number
19: of false positives (78 and 68 events), and the correct-decision percentage
20: stayed at 41% and 43%. The authors conclude that the output is good
21: enough to warn a rider but not to trigger automatic emergency braking.
22: Two points from that study influenced this project. The false
23: positives are a reminder that a predictor with high recall needs a plan for
24: the times it is wrong, which is why minimum gaps and contact outcomes

=== PDF PAGE 24 (595.32 x 841.92) ===
01: 9
02: are reported in simulation here, not just regression error. And an LSTM
03: that reads a time series is a heavier object than a feed-forward network
04: that reads a single state vector. The 4.5 s figure is also worth reading
05: carefully: it measures how far ahead of a collision the prediction was
06: made, not how long the computation took, so it cannot be set against the
07: microsecond inference times reported later.
08: Zou et al. (2023) consider an automated vehicle approaching a
09: pedestrian who is completely hidden by vehicles in the adjacent lane. A
10: baseline strategy is compared with one that uses V2V messages from the
11: neighbouring vehicles. Without V2V, the time to collision is close to
12: zero when the pedestrian is detected, and the vehicle brakes hard but still
13: hits the pedestrian. With V2V it can brake in proportion to the hazard,
14: the time to collision stays larger and the pedestrian crosses safely. The
15: experimental design, a baseline run and a V2V run from identical starting
16: conditions, is the one followed in Chapter 7.
17: Other recent work approaches the same family of problems
18: from different directions. Ben-Mosbah et al. (2024) study the electronic
19: emergency brake light application over 5G NR V2V, the same situation
20: as the braking-chain scenario here but with a cellular sidelink radio.
21: Farhat et al. (2024) propose a cooperative collision-avoidance system for
22: vehicular communication based on deep learning. Park et al. (2025)
23: combine C-V2X with camera-based object detection in a client–server
24: arrangement and report a mean broadcast latency of 9.24 ms for obstacle
25: information on a test track. None of these runs its model on a
26: microcontroller, which is the gap this project tries to fill.

01: 10
02: 2.3 Message dissemination and traffic estimation
03: Li et al. (2022) look at the load that safety messages place on
04: the network. Their end–edge–cloud architecture lets edge nodes predict
05: future vehicle trajectories and the density of vehicles along them. A
06: warning is then sent only to vehicles expected to pass through the
07: affected area, and the system switches between V2V and V2I delivery
08: depending on connectivity. On a real-world VANET mobility dataset it
09: outperformed schemes that ignore the predicted trajectory density. That
10: work is about who should receive a message, whereas this project is
11: about what the receiver does with it. The two fit together, since an
12: estimator can only act on state that is still fresh.
13: Wang et al. (2019) detect road congestion with V2V
14: messages. A fuzzy controller built on vehicle speed and traffic density
15: grades the local congestion level, neighbouring vehicles are asked for
16: theirs, and a hypothesis test over a large sub-sample gives a regional
17: estimate. In simulation the detection accuracy was 5.5% higher than a
18: geomagnetic-coil method and 7.5% higher than the CoTEC method,
19: while the V2V network overhead was 90.8% lower than CoTEC.
20: Congestion and imminent collision are different targets, since a slow,
21: dense queue need not contain any conflict and a collision can arise on an
22: empty road. What carries over from this paper is the idea of computing a
23: compact local estimate on each vehicle and exchanging only what
24: neighbours need.
25: Zardosht et al. (2017) use V2V messages for another purpose,

06: 2.4 Simulation, communication and embedded tools
07: The simulation side of the project rests on a well-established
08: tool chain. SUMO is an open-source microscopic traffic simulator in
09: which each vehicle follows a car-following model on a defined road
10: network (Lopez et al. 2018). Its TraCI interface lets an external program
11: read vehicle state and issue commands at every simulation step (Wegener

14: 2008), and Veins couples it to SUMO so that vehicle motion and
15: wireless events influence each other (Sommer et al. 2011).
16: For the communication layer the project borrows ideas from
17: the U.S. DSRC family. IEEE 802.11p provides the radio, IEEE 1609 the
18: networking services, and SAE J2735 and J2945/1 define the message set
19: and its performance requirements (Kenney 2011). The prototype does not
20: implement these stacks. It uses ESP-NOW, a connectionless protocol
21: from Espressif that sends short frames directly between ESP32 boards
22: without an access point (Espressif Systems n.d.). The choice was a
23: practical one, not a standards-based one, and Chapter 3 explains how
24: closely the prototype follows the standards.

26: shows that neural-network inference can fit in tens of kilobytes (David et
27: al. 2021), and Pohuliai et al. (2026) compare several TinyML models for

10: the fast vehicle, starting at 4.2 m/s with a gap of 1.8 m to the other bot

17: 6.3 Link-quality inputs
18: Three of the model inputs describe the link, and the firmware
19: derives them from the packets themselves. The packet delivery ratio is
20: the number of packets received divided by the number expected, where
21: the expected count grows by the jump in sequence number each time a
22: packet arrives. Message age is the time since the last packet was
23: received. Hop count is read from the packet and is always 1 in the two-
24: board set-up. If nothing has been heard for 2 s, the bot treats the other as
25: silent and substitutes a zero speed, zero acceleration, an age of 250 ms
26: and a single hop.
27: These definitions are adequate for a first demonstration, but
28: they differ from the training data in three ways that will matter in Phase
29: II. First, the firmware PDR is cumulative since start-up, whereas the
30: dataset used a sliding window, so one early loss lowers it for a long time.
31: Second, message age is measured from reception and not from the
32: sender's timestamp, so it leaves out the time the packet spent in transit.

=== PDF PAGE 53 (595.32 x 841.92) ===
01: 38
02: Third, a bot is considered alive during the first 2 s after boot even though
03: no packet has been received, so for that interval the model is fed
04: placeholder values for the other vehicle.
05: The occlusion flag is a heuristic. It is set when the other bot is
06: moving faster than 0.3 m/s and the gap is more than 4.5 m. The ToF
07: sensor assumed in the dataset reaches 4.0 m, so a gap beyond 4.5 m
08: means that the sensor could not have seen the other vehicle while the
09: V2V link could. In the present tests the gap is itself simulated, so the flag
10: shows how the input would be set and does not represent a real sensing
11: event.
12: 6.4 From point-to-point exchange to a broadcast beacon
13: Pairing two boards by MAC address was a conscious
14: simplification. The goal at the end of Phase I was to show that two
15: ESP32 boards could exchange vehicle state over ESP-NOW and use it in
16: the model, and that goal was met: both bots ran the loop for the full
17: duration of the tests and responded to each other's state.
18: A road is not a pair of vehicles, however, and the planned
19: arrangement is different. Each vehicle will broadcast a beacon, and any
20: vehicles within range will receive it and respond. ESP-NOW supports a
21: broadcast address, so the radio itself needs no change, but the firmware
22: will have to be reorganised around it. It will need a table of nearby
23: senders keyed by identity, with PDR and message age kept separately for
24: each; a rule for choosing which neighbour to treat as the conflict vehicle;
25: and filtering by signal strength or distance, so that distant vehicles do not
26: clutter the table. These points are listed as Phase II work in Chapter 8.

04: 8.1 Engineering significance and limitations
05: The project joins a compact model to an embedded
06: cooperative prototype and checks it at each junction. In simulation,
07: cooperative warning changed three of the four outcomes from contact to
08: no contact and widened the margin in the fourth. On the board, the
09: network runs in under a tenth of a millisecond and matches its training-
10: framework reference to about 3 × 10−6. The rest of the chain, from
11: sensing to actuation, has not been profiled.
12: The sweeps are useful checks of behaviour. A wider gap
13: lowers the risk and raises the advised speed, and worse link quality
14: makes the outputs more cautious. They cover only the chosen input
15: conditions, however, and do not establish behaviour over every
16: combination of inputs the network may meet.
17: The control side needs work. The braking comparison still
18: ends in contact, the speed oscillates at close range, and the dashboard
19: example shows the two outputs disagreeing. One contributor in the two-
20: board firmware may be the rate limit: a speed change of at most 0.08 m/s
21: per 100 ms tick allows a deceleration of only 0.8 m/s², while the dataset
22: assumes braking of 1.5 to 3.0 m/s². A controller that cannot brake as hard
23: as the labels assume will under-react to a high risk. This is an
24: observation from the code and has not yet been tested.

02: The prototype does not establish GPS or IMU accuracy, motor
03: response, radio performance on moving vehicles, or the transfer of
04: highway-scale behaviour to RC-scale motion. Safer roads remain the
05: intended application and not something this study measured.
06: 8.2 Proposed supervisory controller
07: A supervisory layer between the model and the actuator is the
08: most direct response to these findings. It would check the learned advice
09: against simple physical and link-quality rules before the vehicle acts on
10: it. This layer has not been implemented.
11: The first rule concerns stale or missing information. The
12: present firmware proceeds without a first beacon, and for the first 2 s
13: after start-up it feeds placeholder values to the model. A supervisor
14: should define a safe state for that situation, such as a speed cap until a
15: valid packet arrives, and a message age beyond which the other vehicle
16: is treated as unknown. The age limit should come from vehicle dynamics
17: and be tested.
18: The second rule links the two outputs: if the risk exceeds a
19: threshold, the commanded speed should not exceed the current speed,
20: whatever the second head says. The third is a limit on acceleration while
21: a hazard is flagged, and the fourth a rate limit that matches the vehicle's
22: real braking capability. Smoothing or hysteresis on the advice could be
23: added to reduce oscillation, though each adds delay and so has to be tried
24: against minimum gap and stopping time.
25: To judge any of this, the existing controller stays as the
26: baseline, and the experiment is repeated from the same initial conditions,
27: communication trace and model weights. Only then can a change in

09: 8.4 Conclusion
10: This work built a complete, if small, chain for cooperative
11: collision estimation. Four highway situations were modelled in SUMO
12: with Veins and OMNeT++, and in three of them the baseline driver
13: collided while the driver with V2V information did not. A bot-scale
14: dataset of 10,000 rows was generated, and an 11–16–16–2 network with
15: 498 parameters was trained on it, reaching test errors of 0.039 for risk
16: and 0.134 m/s for speed.
17: The network was exported as C code and run on an ESP32 at
18: about 57 µs per inference, with a worst-case difference of 3.30 × 10−6
19: from its reference over 1,215 requests and no communication errors.
20: Two boards exchanged state over ESP-NOW at 10 Hz and adjusted their
21: speeds, and a laptop dashboard exercised the model over Wi-Fi.
22: The braking tests set the limit on what can be concluded.
23: Closing speed at contact dropped from 2.7 to 0.2 m/s, but contact still
24: occurred, and earlier than in the open-loop run. Collision avoidance has
25: therefore not been shown, and the independent outputs and the close-
26: range oscillation are the first control issues for Phase II. The next steps
